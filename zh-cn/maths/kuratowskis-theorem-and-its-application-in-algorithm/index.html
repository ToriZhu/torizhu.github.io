Abstract
#

Kuratowski’s Theorem identifies planar graphs by determining the existence of two forbidden subdivisions, K5K_5K5​ and K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​. This paper provides an expansion of Thomassens’ proof with extra explanation, focusing on the method involved Menger’s Theorem and connectivity. It demonstrates certifying algorithm as the application of the theorem, illustrating a linear-time verification algorithm for non-planarity and presenting.

1. Introduction
#

Every student learning topological graph theory eventually hears about Kuratowski’s Theorem, which is one of the most-cited results in this rapidly growing subject [12]. The planar problem was first solved with two forbidden patterns by Kazimierz Kuratowski in 1930, so the theorem is named after him [13]. Planarity is the bridge which connects geometry and combinatorics. Specifically, Kuratowski’s Theorem can be cited as a starting point for planarity in topological graph theory. It simplifies the problem of whether a graph can be drawn without crossings into simple checking for two specific patterns K5K_5K5​ and K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​.

This paper gives the proof of Thomassen’s modern approach, explaining how the theorem is applied to certifying algorithms by planarity testing and extraction in linear time, a tool in optimization. It is written as a lead-in in topological graph theory for beginners who grasp the basic definitions of graph theory.

For the rest of the paper, Section 2 is the preliminary section, which includes all the concepts and terms that ones need to know for the paper. Section 3 gives the proof of Menger’s Theorem, which is essential when proving Kuratowski’s Theorem, through the three cases with the classification of the place of the separator. Section 4 is where we prove Kuratowski’s Theorem with lemmas, based on the proof of Thomassen’s version. Section 5 is the place to show its adaptation in certifying algorithms, providing a future direction of the research.

3. Menger’s Theorem
#

Menger’s Theorem, proved by Karl Menger in 1927, illustrates the connectivity of a graph not only by its separators but also by its paths, which makes it ”one of the cornerstones of graph theory.” [1] In this chapter, by discussing cases through lemmas, we will prove the theorem which contributes to our proof of Kuratowski’s Theorem by guaranteeing the internally disjoint u−vu-vu−v paths in GGG and determining the connected paths between the vertices as shown in Figure 2. It also provides the presence of disjoint paths which are needed to construct the forbidden subdivisions.

Menger’s Theorem: Let G=(V,E)G=(V,E)G=(V,E) be a graph and A,B⊆VA,B\subseteq VA,B⊆V. Then the minimum number of vertices separating AAA from BBB in GGG is equal to the maximum number of disjoint A−BA-BA−B paths in GGG.

4. Planarity to Kuratowski’s Theorem
#

When you are designing a subway network where tunnels must not intersect, the mathematical problem is whether the graphs are able to be drawn without edge crossings. For small graphs, we can try drawings by hand; but for the larger ones, we need a theoretical way to determine planarity. answeredd. The theorem is elegant, but the proof is complicated. In this chapter, with the help of Menger’s theorem and Euler’s theorem, we analyze paths, cycles, planar embedding, connectivity, and construction to show that the obstruction to planarity inevitably contains one of the forbidden patterns.

Kuratowski’s Theorem: A finite graph GGG is planar if and only if it contains no subdivision of K5K_5K5​ or K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​. [3]

Proposition 4.1. A plane graph satisfying ∣V(G)∣≥3|V(G)|\ge3∣V(G)∣≥3 has at most 3n−63n-63n−6 edges. [7]

Proof. Assume there is a graph GGG with vvv vertices (v≥3v\ge3v≥3), eee edges, and fff faces. According to Euler’s Theorem, we have v−e+f=2v-e+f=2v−e+f=2. Because all faces must be bounded by at least 3 edges, we have f≤2e3f\le\frac{2e}{3}f≤32e​. Thus,

v−e+f=2≤v−e3⇒e≤3v−6. v-e+f=2\le v-\frac{e}{3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad e\le3v-6. v−e+f=2≤v−3e​⇒e≤3v−6.

□\square□

Given the restriction in edges, we can now determine the planarity of specific graphs. In particular, we will prove K5K_5K5​ and K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​ are not planar because of the violation of this bound, which serve as the foundation for the necessity of Kuratowski’s theorem.

Lemma 4.2. If the graph GGG contains K5K_5K5​ or K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​ as a subgraph, it is not planar.

Proof. For K5K_5K5​, according to the definition of a complete graph, we have ∣V∣=5|V|=5∣V∣=5 and ∣E∣=10|E|=10∣E∣=10. We test it with Proposition 4.1 to show whether it is planar or not that ∣V∣−6=9<10=∣E∣|V|-6=9<10=|E|∣V∣−6=9<10=∣E∣, so K5K_5K5​ is not planar. Similarly, for K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​, we have ∣V∣=6|V|=6∣V∣=6, ∣E∣=9|E|=9∣E∣=9, and 3∣V∣−6=12>9=∣E∣3|V|-6=12>9=|E|3∣V∣−6=12>9=∣E∣, which is the reason why K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​ is not planar also. [2] □\square□

By applying Lemma 4.2 and the previous proposition, we can prove this lemma. □\square□

5. Application: Planarity Testing
#

When a graph is certified to be nonplanar, we should ensure both efficiency and accuracy. Kuratowski’s Theorem provides not only a simple testing method for planar graphs but also a foundation of algorithms in computational graph theory, illustrating an optimized way to find nonplanar graphs in a shorter period of time. There are two certifying algorithms determining planarity in linear time with the help of Kuratowski’s Theorem: Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm and Boyer-Myrvold algorithm. In this section, we introduce how Kuratowski’s Theorem is involved in certifying verification algorithms, especially utilized in Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm of planarity testing.

5.3 Hopcroft-Tarjan Algorithm
#

Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm, introduced in 1974, was the first algorithm to test the planarity of a graph in linear time. Before the appearance of this algorithm, planarity testing algorithms were either exponential or required restrictive assumptions for the input graphs. Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm established an improvement in efficiency in algorithmic graph theory by constructing an embedding of the input graph through a depth-first search tree. If the algorithm cannot complete the planar embedding because of a conflict, it declares that the graph is not planar. However, Kuratowski’s Theorem provides a theoretical guarantee that the conflict can always be resolved into a subdivision of K5K_5K5​ or K3,3K_{3,3}K3,3​. In addition, the extracted subgraphs can be verified independently by isomorphism, requiring Kuratowski’s Theorem as a certificate validation [14].

We prove its property of linear time to better demonstrate its efficiency and function. The Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm runs in linear time by a combination of depth-first search and path addition structure. Thus, we first prove some lemmas that describe the time in each phase, and finally combine them to obtain the time of the Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm.

Lemma 5.1. The graph GGG can be decomposed into O(∣E∣)O(|E|)O(∣E∣) segments in O(∣V∣+∣E∣)O(|V|+|E|)O(∣V∣+∣E∣).

Proof. The decomposition. For each vertex vvv, the algorithm identifies the back edges and traces the tree path. Each edge of the graph is assigned to one segment, so the traversal of each tree path charges a constant number of times for each edge. Thus, the total time is O(∣V∣+∣E∣)O(|V|+|E|)O(∣V∣+∣E∣). □\square□

If a segment cannot be embedded in any face, the algorithm encounters a conflict, which is the result of two segments disabled to be placed in the planar embedding.

The improvement of the Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm is the Boyer-Myrvold algorithm, which simplifies planarity testing by using an addition approach on edges rather than vertex addition, maintaining a partial embedding and attempting to place each edge on the external face. If an edge cannot be placed, the algorithm leaves a Kuratowski subgraph. However, unlike the function in the Hopcroft-Tarjan algorithm, the Boyer-Myrvold algorithm isolates the Kuratowski subgraphs during the embedding process, which involves native extraction and minimality supported by Kuratowski’s Theorem [15].

5.4 Further Reading
#

For readers interested in the broader context of topological graph theory, the classic textbook is Graph Theory written by Diestel [1]. As an extension of Kuratowski’s Theorem, Robertson-Seymour Theorem demonstrates a generalization by the finite obstruction set applying in arbitrary surfaces where Kuratowski’s Theorem only makes sense for the sphere. A more advanced attempt at graphs embeddings on surfaces is given by Graphs on Surfaces [18] written by Mohar and Thomassen. This extends Kuratowski characterizations to higher dimensional surfaces and discusses the problem of crossing numbers. For the intersection of topological graph theory and algorithms, Combinatorial Optimization written by Schrijver [17] provides an extensive area of planarity in the context of polyhedral combinatorics, including the use of Kuratowski subgraphs as cutting planes for the maximum planar subgraph problem.
